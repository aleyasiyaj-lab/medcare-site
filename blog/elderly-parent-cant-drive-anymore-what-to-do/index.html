Elderly Parent Can't Drive Anymore: What to Do Next

2026-07-23 Medcare

When you realize your elderly parent can't drive anymore, what to do becomes one of the most urgent questions you face. Losing the ability to drive affects nearly every aspect of an older adult's independence, and the healthcare implications are significant. Without reliable transportation, medical appointments get missed, prescriptions go unfilled, and chronic conditions go unmanaged. The good news is that Medicare covers a clinical service designed to help seniors navigate exactly this situation.

If your elderly parent can't drive anymore and you are wondering what to do, the answer may start with Community Health Integration (CHI), a Medicare-covered benefit that provides care coordination for seniors whose medical care is affected by transportation barriers.

Why losing the ability to drive is a healthcare concern

Driving cessation is not just a lifestyle change, it is a health event. Studies show that older adults who stop driving are more likely to experience declines in health, increased social isolation, and a higher risk of entering long-term care. When transportation barriers prevent a senior from accessing medical care, the consequences can include:

  • Missed or delayed medical appointments
  • Unmanaged chronic conditions such as diabetes, hypertension, or heart disease
  • Medication lapses and failure to refill prescriptions
  • Delayed detection of new health problems
  • Increased risk of preventable emergency room visits and hospitalizations

This is why Medicare recognizes transportation as a social determinant of health and created CHI to address it clinically.

What is Community Health Integration?

Community Health Integration (CHI) is a Medicare-covered care-management service introduced in 2024. It is designed for patients whose medical care is being affected by unmet social or environmental circumstances, including transportation difficulties. When a senior's inability to drive is interfering with their ability to attend appointments or follow their care plan, a clinician may determine that CHI is medically appropriate.

How CHI helps when a parent can't drive

Through CHI, a care coordinator works directly with your parent to address the transportation gap created by driving cessation. The coordinator can:

  • Identify local transportation options, including volunteer driver programs, paratransit services, public transit, and specialized medical transport.
  • Help your parent understand eligibility requirements and navigate application or referral processes for each resource.
  • Coordinate with your parent's healthcare providers to ensure appointments are scheduled with transportation logistics in mind.
  • Develop a person-centered plan that connects transportation solutions to your parent's medical care plan.
  • Provide ongoing follow-up to ensure the transportation plan is working and to adjust when new barriers arise.

CHI is a healthcare service, not a cash benefit or a voucher. The coordinator helps your parent access community resources, but each outside organization has its own eligibility rules and makes its own decisions.

The first step: an Annual Wellness Visit

The pathway to CHI begins with an initiating visit, which may be an Annual Wellness Visit (AWV). The AWV is generally available at no cost under Medicare Part B. During this visit, a licensed clinician conducts a health risk assessment that screens for social determinants of health, including transportation needs.

If the clinician determines that the transportation barrier created by your parent's inability to drive is significantly limiting their ability to diagnose or treat your parent, CHI may be initiated. Only a licensed clinician can make this determination.

Practical steps for families

If your elderly parent can't drive anymore and you are deciding what to do, consider these steps:

  • Schedule an Annual Wellness Visit and make sure transportation difficulties are discussed.
  • Document the specific ways that driving cessation has affected your parent's healthcare.
  • Ask the clinician whether CHI might be appropriate given the transportation barriers.
  • Once CHI begins, stay in contact with the care coordinator to stay informed about the transportation plan.
  • Explore local resources such as Area Agencies on Aging and senior center shuttle services in the meantime.

Cost considerations

The AWV is generally free under Medicare Part B. For CHI services, standard Part B deductible and coinsurance typically apply. Your parent's final cost depends on their supplemental coverage, Medicaid enrollment, or Medicare Advantage plan.

Frequently asked questions

What should I do first when my elderly parent can't drive anymore?

The first step is to schedule an Annual Wellness Visit with their healthcare provider. This visit is generally free under Medicare Part B and includes screening for social needs like transportation. If the clinician determines that transportation barriers are affecting your parent's medical care, CHI care coordination may be initiated.

Will my parent lose their independence if they stop driving?

Driving cessation is a significant life change, but CHI care coordination can help maintain your parent's access to healthcare and community resources. A care coordinator works with your parent to develop a person-centered transportation plan that supports their ongoing independence and medical care needs.

How long does CHI care coordination last?

CHI includes ongoing follow-up as part of the service. The care coordinator checks in regularly to confirm that transportation arrangements are working, identifies unresolved barriers, and updates the coordination plan as needed. The duration depends on the patient's individual needs and the clinician's plan of care.

Key takeaways

  • Losing the ability to drive is a healthcare concern, not just a lifestyle change, and can lead to missed appointments and worsening health.
  • CHI provides a care coordinator who helps your parent navigate transportation resources after driving cessation.
  • The process starts with a free Annual Wellness Visit under Medicare Part B.
  • Only a licensed clinician can determine that CHI is medically appropriate.
  • Ongoing follow-up is built into CHI, ensuring the transportation plan adapts to changing needs.

Important: CHI is not a cash benefit, grant, or financial-assistance payment. It is a healthcare service. Resources and approvals from outside organizations are not guaranteed. Each outside organization has its own eligibility rules and makes its own decisions.
